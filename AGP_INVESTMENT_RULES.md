# Asymmetric Growth Portfolio (AGP)

## Purpose

The **Asymmetric Growth Portfolio (AGP)** is a specifically scoped
portfolio segment intended to pursue unusually high returns from major
secular growth trends over a **5--10 year horizon**.

The AGP is expected to represent approximately **10% of the overall
portfolio**.

These rules apply **only to the AGP**. They should not be treated as the
investor's general investment philosophy or automatically applied to
other portfolio decisions.

## Portfolio-Level Constraints

-   **High liquidity:** Investments should be readily tradable, with
    enough market liquidity to enter or exit without material spread or
    slippage.
-   **No leveraged downside:** An individual investment may lose up to
    **100% of the capital invested**, but it must not create losses or
    liabilities beyond the initial investment. Avoid margin, naked short
    positions, or other structures with loss potential exceeding
    invested capital.
-   **5--10 year horizon:** The strategy is designed around long-term
    structural growth rather than short-term price movements.
-   **High upside:** The portfolio is specifically seeking unusually
    large potential returns. As an initial research hurdle, a candidate
    should generally have a plausible path to approximately **5× or
    greater** under successful execution of management's stated
    operating growth plan.
-   **Individual failures are acceptable:** Substantial volatility and
    even complete losses in individual positions are acceptable because
    the AGP is deliberately limited to a minority of the overall
    portfolio.
-   **Thesis diversification matters:** Multiple securities dependent on
    the same underlying event should be treated as correlated
    expressions of one thesis rather than independent bets.

## Core Investment Principle

Seek investments where a major secular change can produce
**transformative growth relative to the company's current size and
valuation**.

The objective is not simply to identify industries that will grow. The
objective is to identify securities where:

1.  the industry or market has a credible path to substantial growth;
2.  the company can capture enough of that growth to transform its
    revenue and earnings;
3.  today's valuation does not already price in most of that success;
    and
4.  current shareholders can retain a meaningful portion of the
    resulting value.

## Evaluation Parameters

### 1. Secular Growth Thesis

Determine whether there is credible evidence that the underlying
industry, market, or economic activity can grow substantially over the
next 5--10 years.

Questions include:

-   What is driving the growth?
-   How large is the current market?
-   How large could it reasonably become?
-   What evidence supports the expected growth?
-   What could prevent or delay that growth?

### 2. Business Understandability

The investment thesis must be understandable and quantifiable.

We should be able to explain:

-   what the company sells;
-   who pays the company;
-   why customers pay for the product or service;
-   how the company makes money;
-   what specifically is expected to drive growth.

Avoid investments whose economics cannot be explained clearly enough to
model.

### 3. Management Operating Growth Targets

Use management's publicly stated **operating targets**, not stock-price
targets or analyst price targets.

Relevant targets include:

-   revenue;
-   operating income or net income;
-   margins;
-   free cash flow;
-   unit volume;
-   production capacity;
-   customers;
-   market share;
-   other measurable operating goals.

Prefer explicit 5--10 year targets from investor presentations, earnings
calls, capital-markets days, SEC filings, and other primary company
sources.

### 4. Maximum Modeled Return

Management's stated growth plan should initially serve as the
**bull-case operating ceiling**, unless compelling evidence supports
exceeding it.

Translate management's targets through:

**Operating target → revenue → margins → earnings/FCF → projected shares
outstanding → reasonable terminal valuation → implied shareholder
return**

Do not assume that revenue growth translates directly into stock-price
growth.

Use reasonable or conservative terminal valuation multiples. Prefer
investments where exceptional returns arise primarily from **earnings or
free-cash-flow growth**, rather than requiring substantial multiple
expansion.

#### Initial AGP Upside Gate

Before conducting expensive research, ask:

> If management successfully achieves its stated 5--10 year operating
> objectives, can today's shareholder plausibly earn approximately 5× or
> more under reasonable valuation assumptions?

If the answer is clearly **no**, generally stop researching the company
for AGP purposes.

A company rejected for insufficient upside may remain on a watchlist
because a lower future stock price could change the calculation.

### 5. Growth Credibility

For companies that pass the upside gate, assess the probability that
management can actually achieve its stated goals.

Consider:

-   industry demand;
-   customer adoption;
-   signed contracts/backlog;
-   production capacity;
-   management execution history;
-   historical guidance accuracy;
-   market-share assumptions;
-   technology readiness;
-   regulatory constraints;
-   supply-chain constraints;
-   required organizational scaling.

### 6. Competitive Position

Determine why this company, specifically, should capture the expected
industry growth.

Consider:

-   intellectual property;
-   technical differentiation;
-   switching costs;
-   network effects;
-   cost advantages;
-   scale;
-   distribution;
-   customer relationships;
-   regulatory barriers;
-   manufacturing capability;
-   data advantages;
-   brand;
-   credible substitutes and competitors.

### 7. Value Capture and Margins

Determine how much revenue growth can become shareholder economic value.

Evaluate:

-   gross margins;
-   operating margins;
-   incremental margins;
-   free-cash-flow conversion;
-   required operating expenses;
-   maintenance capex;
-   pricing power.

Revenue growth that requires proportionally increasing expenses may
create much less equity value than high-incremental-margin growth.

### 8. Capital Requirements

Determine how much capital is required to achieve management's growth
plan.

Questions include:

-   Does growth require new factories, data centers, inventory,
    acquisitions, or other major capital expenditures?
-   Can growth be funded internally?
-   How much external financing may be required?
-   Does management's target implicitly depend on raising substantial
    additional capital?

### 9. Leverage

Evaluate the company's debt burden and financial obligations.

Relevant measures include:

-   total debt;
-   net debt;
-   net debt / EBITDA;
-   interest expense;
-   interest coverage;
-   fixed versus variable rates;
-   covenants;
-   collateral;
-   refinancing exposure.

### 10. Financial Runway / Debt Clock

Debt can put an expiration date on an otherwise correct growth thesis.

Compare the **growth horizon** with the **financial clock**.

Examine:

-   debt maturity schedule;
-   expected refinancing dates;
-   cash balance;
-   cash burn;
-   free cash flow;
-   interest obligations;
-   covenant risk;
-   expected capital needs.

Prefer companies that can survive meaningful delays in their growth
thesis.

A company targeting success in 2031 but facing a liquidity crisis or
major refinancing in 2028 should be penalized because it must be both
**right and on time**.

### 11. Dilution Risk

Determine how much of the future company's value will belong to today's
shareholder.

Consider:

-   historical share-count growth;
-   stock-based compensation;
-   convertible securities;
-   warrants;
-   expected equity financing;
-   acquisition-related issuance;
-   capital requirements likely to require new shares.

Model returns on a **per-share** basis when dilution could be material.

### 12. Current Valuation

Focus on what is being paid **today** relative to plausible future
earning power.

Useful metrics may include:

-   market capitalization;
-   enterprise value;
-   P/E;
-   EV/EBITDA;
-   EV/revenue;
-   price/FCF;
-   valuation relative to expected future earnings.

A great company or rapidly growing industry is not necessarily a good
AGP investment if today's price already assumes extraordinary success.

### 13. Failure-Case Value

Estimate what happens if the secular-growth thesis or management growth
plan fails.

Possible outcomes include:

-   continued value from an existing profitable business;
-   substantial but survivable decline;
-   persistent cash burn and dilution;
-   insolvency;
-   near-total or total loss.

AGP permits a 100% loss, but the failure case should still be explicitly
understood.

### 14. Liquidity

Confirm that the security satisfies the AGP liquidity requirement.

Evaluate:

-   average daily dollar volume;
-   bid/ask spread;
-   exchange;
-   market capitalization/free float;
-   any structural restrictions on trading.

### 15. Thesis Independence

Before adding a candidate to the portfolio, determine how correlated its
success is with existing AGP positions.

Five stocks dependent on the same robotics-adoption scenario should not
be treated as five independent investment theses.

## Important Methodological Rules

### Operating Projections, Not Stock Targets

Management and analyst stock-price targets are not inputs to the AGP
return model.

Use management's statements about the **business**, then independently
calculate what those results could make the equity worth.

### Do Not Require Multiple Expansion

Prefer candidates whose required return can be generated through
underlying business growth.

Multiple expansion may provide additional upside but should not normally
be necessary for the investment to clear the AGP return hurdle.

### Past Price Movement Is Diagnostic

Past stock-price movement is not itself an input to expected five-year
return.

It may, however, reveal that investors have already discovered a thesis
and expanded the valuation multiple.

The primary question is:

> What are we paying today for the company's plausible future cash
> flows?

### Management Targets Are the Initial Bull-Case Ceiling

Do not invent extraordinary growth beyond what the company itself is
planning merely to manufacture a 5× or 10× scenario.

Management's stated operating plan is the initial upper-bound scenario.
Model higher outcomes only when independent evidence provides a
compelling reason.

### Screen Upside Before Probability

Do not spend hours determining whether a company can execute a growth
plan that would provide insufficient returns even if fully achieved.

First calculate the potential payoff.

Then investigate the probability of receiving it.

### Rejections Should Be Retained

Record why every rejected candidate failed.

Useful rejection categories include:

-   insufficient upside at current valuation;
-   insufficient liquidity;
-   excessive leverage;
-   inadequate financial runway;
-   excessive dilution;
-   no quantifiable management target;
-   secular thesis too weak;
-   opportunity too small relative to company;
-   business not sufficiently understandable;
-   competitive position inadequate.

A rejected company can become attractive later if valuation or
fundamentals change.

## Research Funnel

The AGP research process should minimize expensive research on
candidates that can be eliminated cheaply.

### Pass 1 --- Mechanical Screen

Target research time: approximately 2--5 minutes per company or
automated where possible.

Collect:

-   ticker/company;
-   exchange;
-   security type;
-   market cap;
-   enterprise value;
-   revenue;
-   recent revenue growth;
-   net income;
-   free cash flow;
-   cash;
-   debt;
-   basic valuation metrics;
-   average daily dollar volume;
-   industry/sector;
-   possible secular-growth thesis.

Apply hard structural and liquidity exclusions.

### Pass 2 --- Management Target / Upside Screen

Find explicit long-term management operating targets.

Perform a rough model translating those targets into:

-   future revenue;
-   future earnings/FCF;
-   future share count;
-   reasonable terminal valuation;
-   implied return from today's price.

Reject candidates that clearly cannot produce approximately **5×** even
with successful execution.

### Pass 3 --- Financial Survivability

For survivors, analyze:

-   leverage;
-   debt maturities;
-   interest burden;
-   cash runway;
-   FCF;
-   capital requirements;
-   financing needs;
-   dilution.

Determine whether the company can survive long enough to realize the
growth thesis and whether today's shareholders will capture the
resulting value.

### Pass 4 --- Deep Thesis Research

Only strong survivors receive expensive qualitative research.

Analyze:

-   industry growth evidence;
-   management credibility;
-   customer adoption;
-   competitive landscape;
-   moat;
-   execution history;
-   market-share assumptions;
-   regulatory risk;
-   technological risk;
-   detailed failure cases.

## Initial Research Universe

The first systematic candidate universe is **U.S.-listed publicly traded
common equities**, beginning broadly rather than limiting the search to
companies already associated with AI or another fashionable theme.

The objective is to avoid selection bias toward companies already
receiving substantial investor attention.

Potential later universes can include other sufficiently liquid
securities or international equities where they satisfy the AGP mandate.
